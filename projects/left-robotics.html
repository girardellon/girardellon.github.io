Line Following and Evolutionary Obstacle Avoidance

Simulation / Hardware Video
Evorobot Simulator
Webots e-puck

Problem Statement

Reliable mobile-robot navigation depends on both the quality of sensory information and the control strategy used to interpret it. This project examined three complementary approaches: the hardware architecture of an mBot line-following robot, PID-based line tracking for an e-puck in simulation, and evolutionary learning for obstacle avoidance with a Khepera robot. The work addressed the limitations of binary optical sensing, the need for continuous trajectory correction, and the challenge of developing obstacle-avoidance behaviour without explicitly programming the robot’s responses.

Proposed Solution

The mBot hardware was analysed at the sensor, signal-conditioning, microcontroller, and motor-driver levels. The study evaluated the CNY70 reflective sensor array and its binary interface through the HEF40106B Schmitt-trigger stage, calculated suitable component values, and proposed an auxiliary MPU-6050 inertial-sensing board connected to the PIC16F877A over I²C. In Webots, a Python controller was implemented for the e-puck, combining PID line-following with speed modulation based on front proximity-sensor readings. In parallel, a reactive neural controller mapping the Khepera’s infrared sensor inputs to wheel commands was evolved in Evorobot using a genetic algorithm over 100 generations.

Key Results & Contributions

  1. Sensor and hardware analysis: Identified how binary signal conditioning limits the use of proportional control and proposed an MPU-6050-based inertial-sensing board for the mBot. The board is a design proposal, rather than a experimentally validated implementation.
  2. PID line-following: Implemented a Webots e-puck controller that combines ground-sensor error with proportional, integral, and derivative terms, while adjusting wheel speed according to front proximity-sensor readings.
  3. Evolutionary obstacle avoidance: The baseline Khepera controller reached a best fitness of 0.382. Adding five hidden units produced the same final fitness, indicating no measurable benefit from the larger network in the tested setup.
  4. Evolutionary parameter evaluation: Increasing mutation probability from 3% to 10% led to less stable learning and a final fitness of 0.302, while increasing sensor noise to 0.20 reduced final fitness to 0.295, a 22.8% decrease relative to the baseline.
